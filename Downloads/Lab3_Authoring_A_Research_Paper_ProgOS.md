# Part A — Paper Skeleton & General Rules

### A.1 Typical Paper Structure

To begin with, a typical paper structure is listed as follows:

**MAIN METADATA**
- **Title**
- **Authors and Affiliations**
- **Abstract** (short summary, usually 150–250 words)

**MAIN BODY**
- **1. Introduction**
- **2. Related Work**
- **3. Theoretical / Architectural Explanation** (high-level design, background, model, or system architecture)
- **4. Detailed Analysis / Implementation / Evaluation Setup** (lower-level details, algorithms, design decisions, experimental methodology)
- **5. Validation and Tests**
- **6. Conclusions, Limitations, and Future Work**
- **References**

*However*:
- Sections **3 and 4 can sometimes be merged** into a single section, often called **“Design and Implementation”**, **“Approach”**, or **“System Design and Evaluation Setup”**, especially for shorter papers.

***!Remember!***: The **section numbering must be consistent** and logical (no jumping from 2 to 4, etc.).

### A.2 General Writing Rules (for all Sections)

You should follow these simple rules when writing any section:

- Use **short, clear sentences**; avoid very long or overly complex ones.
- Aim for **one main idea per paragraph**.
- Avoid informal language (no slang, no jokes inside the technical text).
- Be **precise** (prefer “The system reduces latency by 15%” rather than “The system is much faster”).
- Avoid copy–paste from any source (papers, websites, AI outputs) and always **rewrite in your own words**.
- **Never** make bold statements without using references (e.g., "Today's society relied heavily on data generated by social media platforms." --> ***This claim needs a reference to/from a trusted source***).
- Keep tense consistent:
  - Use **present tense** to describe the paper itself and general truths (“In this paper, the authors propose…”, “TCP uses congestion control…”).
  - Use **past tense** for experiments and what you actually did (“The authoring team measured…”, “The authors of the current work also implemented…”).

# Part B — Section-by-Section Guidelines

## B.1 Section 1 – Introduction

You should opt for an Introduction that:

- **Motivates the problem**:
  - Explain **why** the topic is important (e.g., performance, security, usability, cost).
  - Give a simple real-world example where this problem appears.
- **Defines the problem** clearly:
  - State what is missing or what is hard in current systems.
  - Be specific, not generic (“buffer overflows in C web servers” instead of “security is important”).
- **States your contributions**:
  - 2–4 bullets that say what the paper does (e.g., “This work presents design X”, “This paper showcases implementation Y”, “An evaluation of Z is presented.”).
  - Each contribution should be **concrete and measurable**, not vague.
- **Includes a Table of Contents (ToC) paragraph at the end** of the Introduction:
  - A short paragraph describing the structure of the paper.
  - Example structure (*to be adapted*):
    - “The rest of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews related work. Section 3 presents the architecture of the proposed system. Section 4 details the implementation and evaluation setup. Section 5 reports the experimental results. Section 6 concludes the paper and discusses limitations and future work.”

## B.2 Section 2 – Related Work

This section **requires real research**, not only generative AI:

- You **must search for publications** using:
  - Google Scholar, ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, arXiv (*not much*), etc.
  - University library search tools.
- You should:
  - Identify *about* **6–10 relevant papers or systems**.
  - For each, write **1–2 sentences** summarizing what it does and how it relates to your idea.
  - Group similar works together in subsections (e.g., “static analysis tools”, “runtime monitoring systems”).

Rules for Related Work:

- Do **not** simply ask a generative AI “give me related work” and copy the answer.
- Use generative AI only to:
  - Help **rephrase** your own summaries.
  - Help **organize** your comparison (e.g., ask for “rewrite these 3 bullet points into a paragraph”).
- You must **check that every cited paper really exists**:
  - Many AI tools “hallucinate” references (invent papers).
  - You must verify authors, title, venue, and year in a real database.
  - You must also **ensure that the papers are truly related to your work**.
- When comparing to existing work:
  - Always explain **how your idea is similar and how it differs**.
  - Use small comparative phrases like “Unlike X, the current approach…”, “Similar to Y, the proposed system also…”.

## B.3 Sections 3 and 4 – Theory / Architecture and Detailed Analysis

### B.3.1 Section 3 – Theoretical / Architectural Explanation

This section provides the **high-level view**:

- You have to describe the **overall architecture** of the system, model, or method:
  - Main components (modules, services, layers).
  - How components interact (data flow, control flow).
- Explain any **theoretical foundations**:
  - Definitions, assumptions, and models (e.g., process model, network model, threat model).
  - High-level algorithms or strategies (but not all code-level details).
- Use **figures or diagrams** (even simple - **but nicely designed** -  boxes and arrows) to show:
  - Data paths.
  - Interactions between components.
- Keep this section at a **conceptual level**:
  - Focus on **what** the system does and **how the pieces fit together**, not implementation details.
  - Leave detailed optimization and parameter tuning to Section 4.

### B.3.2 Section 4 – Detailed Analysis / Implementation / Evaluation Setup

This section goes **deeper into details**:

- **Implementation details**:
  - Main programming languages, libraries and frameworks used.
  - Important data structures and algorithms.
  - Key design decisions and trade-offs.
- **Evaluation setup**:
  - Hardware and software environment (CPU, RAM, OS, network).
  - Dataset description (size, source, preprocessing).
  - Workload description (what is being tested and under what conditions).
- **Configuration and parameters**:
  - Relevant configuration options (e.g., buffer sizes, number of threads, timeouts).
  - Reasonable default values and how they were selected.
- **Reproducibility details**:
  - Enough information so another researcher could **repeat the experiments**.

***Important note:***

- For **shorter papers**, Sections 3 and 4 are often **merged into one combined section**, e.g., “3. Design and Implementation” or “3. Approach”.
- *If you choose to do so*:
  - Start with the high-level architecture.
  - Then gradually move into more implementation and evaluation details in the same section.

## B.4 Section 5 – Validation and Tests

This section explains **how you prove that your idea works** (or why it fails in some cases):

- **Define clear metrics**:
  - For systems work: latency, throughput, CPU usage, memory usage, energy, etc.
  - For correctness: error rate, false positives/negatives, accuracy, etc.
- **Describe experiments**:
  - What exactly did you measure?
  - Under what scenarios (normal load, peak load, failure conditions)?
  - How many runs, how you handle noise/variance.
- **Baselines and comparisons**:
  - Compare against at least one **baseline** (existing system, simpler version, or no optimization).
  - Briefly explain why the baseline is fair and relevant.
- **Results presentation**:
  - Use tables and graphs (even simple ones) to summarize results.
  - Highlight trends, not every single number.
  - *Avoid cherry-picking*; if some results are negative or neutral, mention them honestly.
- **Threats to validity**:
  - Briefly discuss factors that might make results less general:
    - Limited datasets, specific hardware, synthetic workloads, small sample sizes, etc.

---

## B.5 Section 6 – Conclusions, Limitations, and Future Work

This section **wraps up the paper** and provides an honest reflection:

- **Restate the problem and contributions**:
  - Summarize the main achievements in 2–4 sentences.
  - Avoid repeating entire paragraphs from the Introduction.
- **Summarize key results**:
  - Mention the most important 1–3 findings (e.g., “reduced latency by X%”, “improved throughput by Y%”).
- **Limitations**:
  - Be honest about what your approach cannot do:
    - Only tested on small datasets.
    - Only works under certain assumptions.
    - Not yet optimized for production.
- **Future work**:
  - Suggest concrete steps for improvement:
    - Extend to new scenarios or workloads.
    - Improve scalability or security.
    - Integrate with other systems.

# Part C — Responsible Use of Generative AI

When leveraging GenAI, you must always keep in mind the following reminders:

- **Generative AI (e.g., ChatGPT, Copilot) is allowed**, but only as a **support tool**, not as the **sole author** of the paper.
- You **must edit and adapt** any AI-generated text:
  - Rewrite in your own words.
  - Add your own ideas, structure and arguments.
  - Check all technical content for correctness.
- You must **not**:
  - Ask the AI to “write the whole paper” and submit it unchanged.
  - Use AI-generated references without verifying them.
  - Use AI as the only source of “related work”.

***You should know that***:

- **Plagiarism and AI-detection tools (e.g., Turnitin)** can detect text that has been generated and not meaningfully edited.
- Even if detection tools were to fail, using AI as the sole writer:
  - Prevents **you** from actually learning to write and think critically.
  - Violates academic integrity rules of most universities.

*Recommended safe usage patterns*:

- Use AI to:
  - Clarify difficult concepts (“Explain X in simpler words”).
  - Suggest alternative phrasings for text that the you already wrote.
  - Check grammar and basic style.
  - Brainstorm **questions** to investigate, but not the *final* answers.
- Do **not** use AI to:
  - Invent related work or references.
  - Generate entire sections and submit them unchanged.
  - Fake experiments or results.

# Bonus Section — How to Present Your Paper (Slides)

A good (***and accepted for publication***) paper is often accompanied by a **short oral presentation**, typically using a **PPTX (PowerPoint) or similar slide deck**.

Basic guidelines for the presentation:

- **Number of slides**:
  - For a **10–15 minute talk**, aim for about **10–15 slides** (*rule of thumb*: one slide per minute).
- **Suggested slide structure**:
  - Title slide: title, authors, affiliation.
  - Motivation & problem: 1–2 slides.
  - Contributions: 1 slide with bullet points.
  - High-level architecture / approach: 3–4 slides (with simple diagrams).
  - Key results: 3–4 slides with clear charts or tables.
  - Limitations & future work: 1-2 slides.
  - Conclusion: 1 slide summarizing the main message.
- **Design tips**:
  - Use **large fonts**; slides must be readable from the back of the room.
  - Avoid paragraphs of text --> prefer **short bullets** and visuals.
  - Use consistent colors and fonts **and** avoid noisy backgrounds.
  - Each slide should communicate **one main idea**.
- **Presentation delivery**:
  - Do not read slides word-for-word. Use them as **prompts**.
  - Practise timing. Ensure you can finish comfortably within the given slot.
  - Be prepared to answer **simple questions** about your work.
